In CMU's **16-665 Robot Mobility**, I took an F1TENTH car from "don't hit the wall" to planning its own way around obstacles. Each lab added one capability. Each one had to work on the real car, not just in simulation.

## How I tested

Every step followed the same loop.

1. **Build it in simulation.** Tune it there and measure it with a metric that doesn't depend on luck.
2. **Move it to the car.** The car has different topics, a noisy pose estimate and real actuator limits.
3. **Find what broke, fix it, and run again.** Most of the fixes below came from this step.

Safety was layered on the car. It only drove itself while a person held a deadman button. The command multiplexer always ranked the human's controls above the autonomy stack, so a person could take over instantly. An emergency-braking node sat between the planner and the motors.

## Step 1: Don't crash

<YouTube id="lU56cSESrSQ" title="On the car: PID wall following through the turns" vertical />

**Emergency braking.** For every LiDAR beam, the node computes the time to collision. If the smallest one drops below 0.5 s, the car stops. In simulation it drove 18 m at 2 m/s with no false brakes, then stopped 0.74 m short of a wall.

**Wall following.** A PID controller holds the car 1.0 m from the wall, using two LiDAR beams to find the wall's angle and distance.

**What hardware testing caught:**

- **The steering gain did nothing.** Changing it had no effect, because the servo command fell outside its allowed range and was clamped. Lowering the gain's magnitude brought it back into range.
- **The car ran the wrong code.** "Not working" was often the C++ skeleton running instead of the Python node, or two nodes publishing drive commands at once.
- **The braking node had to sit in the command path.** The wall follower publishes to a separate topic, and only commands the safety node clears reach the wheels.

## Step 2: Avoid obstacles

Follow-the-gap is a reactive planner. It has no map and no memory. It uses only the current scan. It grows every obstacle by the car's radius, finds the widest reachable gap and steers toward its center.

**What testing caught:**

- **A wrong guess, caught by measuring.** The car kept reversing in a cluster of boxes. I assumed the robot radius was too large, but shrinking it caused collisions. The real cause was two safety margins stacking up. Together they equalled the 0.45 m gap between the boxes, so the gap vanished.
- **The LiDAR saw the car itself.** On hardware, four beams hit the car's own chassis at about 0.17 m. The usable forward view shrank from 3.94 m to 0.51 m. Ignoring returns closer than 0.20 m fixed it.

## Step 3: Know where you are and follow a line

<YouTube id="r8tLKzRWAZ4" title="Full hardware run: pure pursuit with particle-filter localization" />

In simulation the car's pose is perfect. On the real car, a particle filter estimates it by matching each LiDAR scan against a map of the building. Everything downstream has to cope with a pose that is always a little wrong.

Pure pursuit steers toward a point on a recorded line, a fixed lookahead distance ahead. I swept the lookahead from 0.3 m to 2.0 m in simulation and measured how close the car came to the walls. Shorter was always safer in simulation. I still chose 0.6 m for the car, because a short lookahead amplifies pose noise from the particle filter.

I also didn't use lap time to compare settings. The same configuration measured 15.5 s and 20.4 s on two runs, so lap time was mostly noise. Wall clearance was a far more stable metric.

## Step 4: Plan around obstacles

<Clip
  src="/projects/f1tenth-rrt-hardware.mp4"

The car follows its recorded line until the LiDAR shows the route is blocked. Then it plans a detour with RRT, flies it, and returns to the line.

- **A live map from the LiDAR.** Each scan becomes an occupancy grid 7 m ahead and 4 m to each side. Obstacles are grown more along the car's length than its width, to match its shape.
- **Tested against ground truth.** I checked 25 simulated runs against the true map, not the planner's own map. On a tight first layout, 20 of 25 laps were clean. All the failures were at two spots with only 0.05 m to spare. On a layout with at least 0.4 m to spare, 15 of 15 laps were clean.
- **A bug fixed by deleting code.** The detour was planned for the car's body center, then shifted back to its reference point. At sharp corners that shift once pushed the path into an obstacle. Steering toward the body-center path directly removed the problem.

## What moving to hardware taught me

- **Pose stops being free.** The simulator gives perfect odometry. The car only has a noisy estimate, on a different topic. Every controller had to work with both.
- **The sensor sees the car.** Self-hits silently shrank the LiDAR's view until I filtered them out.
- **Actuators have limits you don't choose.** A gain change that "does nothing" is often a clamped output.
- **Process hygiene matters.** Two copies of the hardware bringup fight over the motor controller. Many "the car won't move" sessions were process problems, not algorithm problems.
