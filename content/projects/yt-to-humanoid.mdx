Humanoid robots are increasingly taught by watching people. This project runs that idea end to end with NVIDIA's open humanoid stack: **a normal video goes in, and a simulated Unitree G1 reproduces the motion** — balancing on its own, with no motion capture and no robot-specific retargeting.

GEM-X (pose from video) and SONIC (the whole-body controller) are NVIDIA's released models; I did not train them. My work is everything around them — the pipeline that connects them, the simulation setup that runs them reproducibly, and the measurement tools that show how well the robot really follows its input.

## The pipeline

```
video → GEM-X (3D human pose) → SMPL joints → ZMQ stream → SONIC (whole-body policy) → Unitree G1 in MuJoCo
```

1. **Perception.** GEM-X detects and tracks the person, estimates 2D keypoints, and lifts them to a 3D body pose for every frame.
2. **Conversion.** The pose is converted into the 24-joint SMPL skeleton the controller expects.
3. **Streaming.** Frames are published over ZMQ on an exact real-time schedule.
4. **Control.** SONIC — a learned policy that maps human motion directly to robot joint targets at 50 Hz — keeps the 29-DoF G1 balanced while it imitates.
5. **Simulation and recording.** The G1 runs in MuJoCo; the input stream and the robot's joint state are logged on one clock for analysis.

## End-to-end policies

SONIC is an end-to-end whole-body controller: it observes the human skeleton and outputs every joint target directly, with the human-to-robot mapping learned inside the policy rather than hand-written. Getting it running meant building its C++ deployment with TensorRT on new GPUs, verifying it against NVIDIA's reference motions (control loop ≈ 65 µs per step, zero errors), and then feeding it a stream it had never seen: pose estimated from internet video.

## Simulation

- **Headless and scripted.** The MuJoCo simulator runs on a virtual display on a remote GPU machine, viewable live in a browser, and is started, driven and stopped entirely from scripts — including releasing the safety harness that holds the robot up at start.
- **Re-rendered, synchronized views.** The front and side videos are rendered from the robot's logged state, so both show the identical motion, aligned with the original by the measured latency.

## Data pipeline

- **Video preparation** into clean, constant-frame-rate clips — variable frame rates silently broke pose estimation.
- **Precompute, then replay on a fixed clock.** NVIDIA's offline replay ran a 24 fps clip at 14 fps because conversion time added to every frame. Converting once and publishing on an absolute schedule fixed it: 1,200 frames in 39.97 s, one late frame in the whole run.
- **A small fix upstream code needed.** NVIDIA's pose converter crashed on the current body-model release; a 5-line patch, with unit tests, fixed it.
- **Reproducible setup:** third-party repos pinned to exact commits, one config file, and scripts for every stage.

## Evaluation

The robot "looks like it's dancing" is not a result, so I measured it.

- **Stability:** no falls in any of 7 replays across 3 clips.
- **Tracking:** on the demo clip, human and robot limb angles correlate at 0.8–0.9 for arms and legs and about 0.6 for torso lean, with a mean error of 11.5°.
- **Latency:** about 0.4 s from input to robot, found by the delay that best aligns the two motions (correlation rises from 0.59 to 0.86).
- **Systematic biases:** on every clip the robot holds its arms 15–25° higher and stands more upright than the person — a property of the controller, not noise.
- **Finding the real bottleneck.** On a yoga clip the robot held its hands far apart during a prayer pose. Checking each stage showed the skeleton it received was already wrong — 0.6 m between hands that were touching. The error came from perception: from a side-on camera, a single view can't see how far apart the hands are. Frontal clips looked much more plausible.
