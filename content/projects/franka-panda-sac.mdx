## The problem

Color-based pick-and-place with a 7-DOF Franka Panda: see a colored object,
grasp it, move it, place it. Easy to state, hard to learn — especially when the
simulator's contact physics are only approximately right.

## Motivation

I wanted to test whether **physics priors** — specifically Lagrangian structure —
could make reinforcement learning more sample-efficient on a manipulation task.
The honest headline: the priors didn't work the way I hoped, and the story of
*why* is the most useful thing here.

## Architecture

A hierarchical, two-tier controller:

1. **High-level SAC controller** — learns task sequencing
   (Approach → Grasp → Transport → Place) from a 42-D state vector.
2. **Low-level motion controllers** — execute joint-space trajectories under
   safety constraints.
3. **Physics-informed layer** — the part that got interesting (see below).

Stack: ROS Noetic + Gazebo 11, PyTorch, OpenCV for perception.

## The decision that didn't pan out

I first tried **Deep Lagrangian Networks (DeLaN)** — encoding the manipulator's
Lagrangian directly. In practice the symbolic Lagrangian equations caused
**numerical instability** during training. I pivoted to a **residual RL** approach
instead, which was more robust, though far from a clean win.

## Results — stated honestly

- **~30% success** over 20,000 training episodes.
- Gazebo's limited contact fidelity forced **extensive domain randomization**
  just to get consistent behavior.
- The reward curve is jagged and convergence is fragile.

## Lessons learned

- Physics priors are not free — the numerics of encoding structure can cost you
  more than the sample-efficiency you're chasing.
- Simulator contact fidelity is a first-class variable, not a detail.
