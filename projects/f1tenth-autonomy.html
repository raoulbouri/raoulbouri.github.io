F1TENTH Autonomous Racing (CMU 16-665)

A 1/10-scale race car, taken from emergency braking to 3rd place in the race, with an 8-second best lap at up to 5 m/s. Includes real-car system ID, a sim-to-real PPO policy and a classical race stack.

A full ROS 2 autonomy stack for a 1/10-scale race car, with end-to-end planning by reinforcement learning and classical motion planning on the same vehicle
Hardware
Hokuyo UST-10LX 2D LiDAR · VESC motor controller, IMU gyro and wheel-speed telemetry · steering servo · NVIDIA Jetson Orin

In CMU's 16-665 Robot Mobility, I took an F1TENTH car from "don't hit the wall" to racing. Each lab added one capability, and each one had to work on the real car, not just in simulation. Those labs became the foundation for the final race, where my team built two complete autonomy stacks and took the more reliable one to race day.

Part 1: The foundations

The four labs below built the skills and components the race depended on. Every step followed the same loop.

  1. Build it in simulation. Tune it there and measure it with a metric that doesn't depend on luck.
  2. Move it to the car. The car has different topics, a noisy pose estimate and real actuator limits.
  3. Find what broke, fix it, and run again. Most of the fixes below came from this step.

Safety was layered on the car. It only drove itself while a person held a deadman button. The command multiplexer always ranked the human's controls above the autonomy stack, so a person could take over instantly. An emergency-braking node sat between the planner and the motors.

Step 1: Don't crash

Emergency braking. For every LiDAR beam, the node computes the time to collision. If the smallest one drops below 0.5 s, the car stops. In simulation it drove 18 m at 2 m/s with no false brakes, then stopped 0.74 m short of a wall.

Wall following. A PID controller holds the car 1.0 m from the wall, using two LiDAR beams to find the wall's angle and distance.

What hardware testing caught:

  • The steering gain did nothing. Changing it had no effect, because the servo command fell outside its allowed range and was clamped. Lowering the gain's magnitude brought it back into range.
  • The car ran the wrong code. "Not working" was often the C++ skeleton running instead of the Python node, or two nodes publishing drive commands at once.
  • The braking node had to sit in the command path. The wall follower publishes to a separate topic, and only commands the safety node clears reach the wheels.

Step 2: Avoid obstacles

Follow-the-gap is a reactive planner. It has no map and no memory. It uses only the current scan. It grows every obstacle by the car's radius, finds the widest reachable gap and steers toward its center.

What testing caught:

  • A wrong guess, caught by measuring. The car kept reversing in a cluster of boxes. I assumed the robot radius was too large, but shrinking it caused collisions. The real cause was two safety margins stacking up. Together they equalled the 0.45 m gap between the boxes, so the gap vanished.
  • The LiDAR saw the car itself. On hardware, four beams hit the car's own chassis at about 0.17 m. The usable forward view shrank from 3.94 m to 0.51 m. Ignoring returns closer than 0.20 m fixed it.

Step 3: Know where you are and follow a line

In simulation the car's pose is perfect. On the real car, a particle filter estimates it by matching each LiDAR scan against a map of the building. Everything downstream has to cope with a pose that is always a little wrong.

Pure pursuit steers toward a point on a recorded line, a fixed lookahead distance ahead. I swept the lookahead from 0.3 m to 2.0 m in simulation and measured how close the car came to the walls. Shorter was always safer in simulation. I still chose 0.6 m for the car, because a short lookahead amplifies pose noise from the particle filter.

I also didn't use lap time to compare settings. The same configuration measured 15.5 s and 20.4 s on two runs, so lap time was mostly noise. Wall clearance was a far more stable metric.

Step 4: Plan around obstacles

The car follows its recorded line until the LiDAR shows the route is blocked. Then it plans a detour with RRT, flies it, and returns to the line.

  • A live map from the LiDAR. Each scan becomes an occupancy grid 7 m ahead and 4 m to each side. Obstacles are grown more along the car's length than its width, to match its shape.
  • Tested against ground truth. I checked 25 simulated runs against the true map, not the planner's own map. On a tight first layout, 20 of 25 laps were clean. All the failures were at two spots with only 0.05 m to spare. On a layout with at least 0.4 m to spare, 15 of 15 laps were clean.
  • A bug fixed by deleting code. The detour was planned for the car's body center, then shifted back to its reference point. At sharp corners that shift once pushed the path into an obstacle. Steering toward the body-center path directly removed the problem.

What moving to hardware taught me

  • Pose stops being free. The simulator gives perfect odometry. The car only has a noisy estimate, on a different topic. Every controller had to work with both.
  • The sensor sees the car. Self-hits silently shrank the LiDAR's view until I filtered them out.
  • Actuators have limits you don't choose. A gain change that "does nothing" is often a clamped output.
  • Process hygiene matters. Two copies of the hardware bringup fight over the motor controller. Many "the car won't move" sessions were process problems, not algorithm problems.

Part 2: The race, learning-based stack

real-car bags → system ID → simulator twin → domain randomization → PPO fine-tuning → 40 Hz ROS 2 node

The problem as ML. The policy is an end-to-end planner. A GRU network maps 180 LiDAR ranges and the previous speed directly to steering and speed at 40 Hz. Training maximizes a reward that trades progress against safety: 0.01 per metre of progress, with a -3 penalty and episode end on collision.

Distribution shift. Pretrained imitation-learning and RL policies from End2Race both lapped reliably in the standard simulator. Under realistic actuator dynamics, the behavior-cloned policy fell from 20 of 20 laps to 0, and the PPO policy from 20 to 2. Ablations traced the failure to the roughly 110 ms steering response: small errors compound once the policy leaves the states it trained on. That finding set the plan: model the real car, then retrain under its dynamics.

System identification. We recorded 20 ROS 2 bags on our own car and fit its speed response, delays, dead band, acceleration limits, curvature gain and steering response. The test protocol was locked in git before a held-out bag was opened. On that bag, in forward driving at 0.6 to 1.7 m/s, speed error was 0.039 m/s and yaw-rate error was 0.105 rad/s at a 0.5 s horizon.

Domain randomization. Every training episode samples a new plant, with ranges taken from the real data. It varies correlated speed and yaw parameters, sensing delay, control timing, LiDAR noise and dropouts, laser offset and start conditions. Training also used 152 augmented maps and narrow race-spec tracks.

Fine-tuning in PyTorch. We started from the pretrained End2Race actor, added a fresh critic and trained with recurrent PPO, using GAE, critic warm-up and a target-KL guard. One training run on the real-car model used 7.6 million transitions. On a fitted vehicle model, across 64 held-out starts on the training maps, laps completed rose from 5 to 64 and collisions fell from 59 to 0.

Custom perception model. The policy expects a 360° scan, but the car's LiDAR sees 270°. We trained a 1D CNN on 138,198 paired frames, with a custom Huber loss in the policy's input space and train and test sets split by map and start. It cut rear-sector error from 11.5 m to 0.90 m and raised laps from as low as 50 of 64 to 64 of 64 in simulation.

Evaluation. Comparisons used paired scenes and seeds, preregistered decision rules and bootstrap confidence intervals, plus ablations to separate causes.

Deployment. The policy runs as a 40 Hz ROS 2 node with NumPy-only inference, at about 1.3 ms per decision. It has hold-to-run engagement, input watchdogs and automatic stops, and we tested it on the car in low-speed floor trials.

Part 2: The race, classical stack

We chose this stack for race day because it performed better in qualifiers. It grew directly out of the labs: the particle filter, pure pursuit and reactive driving all came back in faster, more robust forms.

Trajectory optimization. We mapped the track and solved a box-constrained quadratic program for the minimum-curvature line, subject to wall-clearance constraints. A grip-limited speed profile then applies the car's measured acceleration, braking and lateral limits.

Progress vs. safety. A single speed setting exposed the tradeoff in simulation. Raising it from 0.8 to 1.0 cut lap time from 17.6 s to 14.3 s, but nearly doubled peak tracking error from 0.11 m to 0.20 m on a track with about 0.1 m to spare.

Tracking. Pure pursuit follows the line with speed-based lookahead. The 24 Hz particle-filter pose is propagated with the IMU gyro to compensate for pose age and steering delay. A measured speed map converts target speed into motor commands.

Race behaviour. A supervisor makes one decision per LiDAR scan:

  • RACE: follow the raceline at the profile speed.
  • AVOID: detect static obstacles against the map and shift the line into the wider free gap.
  • FOLLOW: track an opponent's speed and hold a safe gap.
  • FALLBACK: switch to a map-free disparity extender if localization goes stale.

Other agents are clustered from LiDAR, tracked between scans and classified as static or moving from their estimated speed along the raceline. We also implemented a Frenet lattice planner that samples and scores candidate trajectories on deviation, consistency and clearance, plus Stanley and kinematic MPC controllers.

Results.

  • In the race: 3rd place, with a best lap of 8 seconds and up to 5 m/s measured on the car.
  • Race laps in simulation: 5 of 5 runs clean on our car model, with realistic pose noise.
  • Obstacle test in simulation: 36 of 36 runs clean at qualification pace.
  • Head-to-head in simulation: 0 collisions in 5 runs.
