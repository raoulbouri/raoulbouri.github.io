Research Direction

Robot learning that holds up outside the simulator.

I'm drawn to the gap between what robot learning achieves in papers and what survives on real hardware. The question running through my work: when should a robot trust its own model, and what should it do when it can't?

  1. 01

    World models

    Learning dynamics good enough to plan and train policies against. Validating my bipedal walker's digital twin showed how one missing physical term makes a simulator confidently wrong. Learned world models deserve the same scrutiny against real hardware.

  2. 02

    Latent representations for safety

    Using the structure of a learned latent space to tell when a robot is outside what it has seen, so uncertainty becomes something the robot can act on. My contact-estimation work does a small version of this: a Kalman filter turns model error into a usable force signal.

  3. 03

    Continual learning in end-to-end policies

    Policies that keep adapting after deployment without forgetting what they already know. At OLA I built drift detection because production data never stops changing. Robots face the same drift in payloads, friction, and wear.

  4. 04

    Generalizing across tasks and embodiments

    Scaling policies to new tasks and new robot bodies instead of retraining for each one. Rebuilding deep RL from scratch taught me how easily a healthy reward curve hides narrow behavior, so generalization has to be measured on real tasks.

What I enjoy most is taking recent methods off the page and onto hardware. The systems engineering training in CMU's MRSD program gives that a discipline: clear requirements, verifying each subsystem, and validating before deployment, so a new method is tested against the robot, not just a benchmark.
